Must-remember rules

  • 1 listed equity option contract usually = 100 shares. Premium $1.50 ≈ $150 per contract.
  • Long call = right to buy. Long put = right to sell (often compared to insurance).
  • Buyers pay a debit (max loss ≈ premium). Sellers receive a credit (risk depends on whether the short is covered or a spread).
  • Break-even is at expiry, before fees: call ≈ strike + premium; put ≈ strike − premium.

What the concepts really mean

A call is the right to buy; a put is the right to sell. Buying buys a right — you choose whether to use it. Selling sells an obligation — you must perform if assigned. That split is the foundation.

Every option price has two parts. Intrinsic value is what the option is worth if exercised right now (only in-the-money options have any). Time value is the rest: the market's price for the chance that things change before expiry. Time value decays to zero — that daily erosion is theta: buyers race the clock while sellers get paid to wait.

Implied volatility (IV) is the market's forecast of future movement, baked into the price. The classic trap: buying just before earnings means buying at peak IV; when the news lands, IV collapses ("IV crush") and the option can lose value even if you called the direction right.

Greeks in one line: Delta — price move per $1 of stock. Gamma — how fast delta changes (highest near expiry). Theta — daily decay. Vega — sensitivity to IV.

Worked examples with real numbers

Assume XYZ stock is at $50; ignore commissions.

Long call

Buy the $55 strike call for $2.00 ($200 out). Break-even at expiry: $57. At $65, the call is worth ($65 − $55) × 100 = $1,000 — an $800 profit. At or below $55, the $200 is gone. Key math: the stock must rise over 14% just to break even — strike and timing matter.

Cash-secured put

Sell the $45 strike put for $1.50 ($150 in) with $4,500 cash ready. Break-even: $43.50. Above $45 at expiry, you keep the $150 (~3.3% on the cash set aside). If assigned, you buy 100 shares at $45 — effectively $43.50 after premium. Fine if you wanted the stock anyway; painful if it gaps to $30.

Iron condor

Stock at $100: sell the $95 put and $105 call, buy the $90 put and $110 call. Collect $2.00 ($200). Max profit $200 if the stock finishes between $95 and $105. Max loss: $5 wing width − $2 credit = $3.00 ($300). Break-evens: $93 and $107. You risk $300 to make $200 — the win rate must compensate.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Forgetting ×100 — the most expensive habit here. Multiply every quote first.
  • Treating credit as profit — the premium is yours only if the position expires well or you close it cheaper.
  • Buying before earnings — IV crush can erase the position even on a correct call.
  • Illiquid options — wide bid-ask spreads start you 5–10% underwater.
  • No plan into expiration week — gamma accelerates; small moves swing the position hard.
  • Selling undefined-risk premium for "income" — a short strangle can collect $300/month and lose $3,000 in a day.

Frequently asked questions

Why multiply by 100?

One standard US equity contract = 100 shares, so $2.50 = $250. Some index products differ — check the specs.

When is my break-even?

At expiry: call strike + premium; put strike − premium. Before expiry, leftover time value can put you in profit below that line.

What if I do nothing at expiration?

ITM options are usually auto-exercised: a long call becomes 100 long shares (you pay strike × 100); a long put sells your shares or opens a short stock position. OTM options die worthless. Know your broker's cutoff times.

Can I close early?

Yes — buy back what you sold or sell what you bought, any time before expiry.

What is assignment risk?

Being short means the buyer can exercise early (common near dividends). Short-put assignment = waking up owning 100 shares; short call = shares called away (or short stock).

Commissions included?

No — subtract them from every estimate; they bite hardest on small and multi-leg trades.
