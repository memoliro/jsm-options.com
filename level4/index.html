Cash-secured puts, calendars, credits, covered calls & LEAPS

More structure, more time. Many discretionary traders prefer 45+ days to expiry so theta is slower at entry and there is room to manage.

Why target 45+ DTE?

  • Slower early decay — theta is often gentler far from expiry than in the final 2 weeks.
  • Room to adjust — roll, close, or hedge before gamma spikes near expiration.
  • LEAPS (long-dated options, often 1+ year) behave more like leveraged stock views with time still embedded.
This is a common teaching guideline, not a rule. Weekly options and 0-DTE products exist and behave very differently (high gamma, fast theta).

Bullish-to-neutral · income · defined at strike

Cash-secured put (CSP)

You sell a put and keep enough cash to buy 100 shares at the strike if assigned. You collect a credit up front. You are modestly bullish to neutral: you want the stock to stay above the strike through expiry (put expires worthless) or you are willing to own shares at an effective price of roughly strike − premium.

  • Max profit = premium received (credit × 100) if the put expires OTM
  • Max loss (if stock goes to zero) ≈ strike × 100 − premium received — still large; “secured” means cash is reserved, not that risk is small
  • Breakeven ≈ strike − premium per share
  • Assignment → you buy shares at the strike; your cost basis is reduced by the premium kept
Example: Stock $100. Sell the $95 put for $2.00 → $200 credit. Set aside $9,500 cash. If stock stays above $95, put expires; you keep $200. If assigned at $95, effective buy price ≈ $93. Risk remains if shares fall further.

Why prefer longer DTE (e.g. 45+)?

  • More premium per unit of risk — very short puts often pay little unless the strike is aggressive
  • Room to manage — roll out in time, adjust strike, or close early before high gamma near expiry
  • Slower early theta on long-dated is less relevant for short puts than for long options; still, many income traders target ~30–45+ DTE so they are paid for time and can exit around 50–75% of max profit
  • Avoid “lottery” weeklies until you understand assignment, gaps, and earnings — the Advanced section encourages learning CSPs on longer-dated, liquid strikes first

Risk: A CSP can lose far more than the credit if the stock collapses. Only use cash you can actually deploy to own the shares. Educational only — not advice.

Income cycle · CSP + covered call

The Wheel strategy

The Wheel is not a single option leg — it is a loop that connects cash-secured puts and covered calls. You collect premium while waiting to own shares you are willing to hold, then collect more premium while you own them, until the shares are called away — then you start over.

  1. Sell a cash-secured put on a stock you are willing to own. If it expires OTM, keep the premium and sell another put. If assigned, you buy 100 shares at the strike (cost basis ≈ strike − premiums kept).
  2. Sell a covered call against those shares. If the call expires OTM, keep the premium and sell another call. If assigned, the shares are called away at the call strike.
  3. Restart — with cash again, return to step 1 and sell a new CSP (same name or another underlier you accept owning).
Why people study it: each step is a structure you already know (CSP + covered call). The “wheel” is the sequence: income while waiting for shares → income while holding shares → shares leave → income again.
Risk stays real: a falling stock still hurts (as the put seller / share owner). A strong rally can call shares away and cap upside. Premium does not make assignment “free.” Educational only — not a recommendation to run the wheel.

Debit vs credit

Debit spreads vs credit spreads

Debit spread

You pay net premium (e.g. bull call, bear put). Max loss ≈ debit. Max gain ≈ width − debit.

Bull call (debit) → Bear put (debit) →

Credit spread

You receive net premium (e.g. bull put, bear call). Max gain ≈ credit. Max loss ≈ width − credit.

Example idea: sell $95 put / buy $90 put for a credit — defined risk if the stock stays above the short strike.

Calendar spread (horizontal)

Same strike, different expiries: typically sell a nearer-term option and buy a longer-dated one (net debit). You want the front month to decay faster while the back month keeps value — often best when price stays near the strike and IV doesn’t crush the long leg unfairly.

Sell 30-DTE $100 call, buy 90-DTE $100 call. Watch the Builder with mixed DTEs and advance “Days Remaining.”

Stock + short call

Covered call (income overlay)

Own 100 shares, sell 1 call against them. You collect premium (“rent”) and cap upside above the short strike. Downside on the shares remains — the premium only cushions losses slightly.

  • Often used when neutral-to-mildly bullish on a stock you already hold
  • Prefer liquid strikes and enough DTE that you are paid for the risk (many use ~30–45 DTE as a teaching range)

Long-dated

LEAPS strategies

LEAPS are long-term equity anticipation securities — options with expirations often a year or more out. Traders use them to:

  • Express a multi-month/year directional view with less capital than 100 shares (still can lose 100% of premium)
  • Sell shorter calls against a LEAPS long call (a diagonal / poor-man’s covered call style idea)
Buy a 12–18 month call (LEAPS) as a surrogate for stock; optionally sell nearer calls against it to reduce cost basis — manage early assignment and dividend risk carefully in live markets.

In the Builder: set DTE to 365+, pick a call, and experiment. For a diagonal, use two call legs with different DTEs (similar to calendar, different strikes optional).
