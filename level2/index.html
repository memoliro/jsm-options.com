Long Call, Long Put, Covered Call — with real numbers

Why a few hundred dollars of premium can control $10,000 of stock exposure — and where the risk still sits.

Bullish

Long Call

Pay premium; need the stock above roughly strike + premium by expiry to profit.

Stock $100. Buy $105 call for $3 → $300 debit. At $110 expiry: ($5 − $3) × 100 = +$200. Max loss: $300.

Long Put

Covered call — collecting “rent”

Own 100 shares at $100. Sell a $110 call for $2 → $200 credit. If shares stay under $110, you keep premium + shares. If they rally past $110, shares may be called away at $110 — upside is capped; downside on the stock remains.

  • Often used when you expect flat to mildly up price action
  • Does not eliminate loss if the stock crashes

Why everything is ×100

One equity option contract usually multiplies by 100 shares. A $3 quote is about $300 cash. The Builder always uses this multiplier so P/L matches brokerage-style dollars.

Greeks in plain English

The Builder shows live Greeks. Here’s what they mean for a position:

  • Delta (Δ) — approx. $ change if the stock moves $1. Positive ≈ bullish bias; negative ≈ bearish.
  • Gamma (Γ) — how fast delta changes. High gamma near the money near expiry = P/L can accelerate.
  • Theta (Θ) — expected $ change per day from time alone. Long options often show negative theta (decay hurts); short premium often positive.
  • Vega (ν) — $ change if implied volatility rises 1 point. Long options usually like higher IV; short options usually don’t.
Open any template in the Builder and move price, days remaining, or IV — watch delta/theta/vega update.
Common mistakes: treating premium quotes as total cash (forget ×100); ignoring that long options can go to zero; selling naked calls without understanding theoretically unlimited risk.
