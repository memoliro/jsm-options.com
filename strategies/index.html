How to choose a strategy

1. What is my market outlook?

Four views cover almost everything, and each card's color tag matches one: bullish (expect a rise), bearish (expect a fall), neutral (expect a range), or volatile (expect a big move, direction unknown). "Neutral" and "volatile" can share the same starting view — a short straddle and a long straddle both say "no directional call" — but one needs price to stay put and the other needs a big move.

2. What is my goal?

Income: collect premium by mostly selling options — covered calls, cash-secured puts, credit spreads, iron condors. Decay works for you; profits are capped and sharp moves hurt. Speculation: profit from a predicted move by mostly buying — long calls, long puts, debit spreads. You pay premium; the move must be big and fast enough. Hedge: you own stock and want to cap damage — protective puts and collars are insurance that costs money and drags on rallies, but limits the fall.

3. How much can I lose on this one position?

Defined-risk strategies — long options, debit spreads, iron condors — state the maximum loss before you enter. Undefined-risk positions — naked short calls or puts, short straddles — can lose far more than the credit collected. Until you understand assignment and margin, stay with defined risk.

When each family shines

Directional debit plays (long calls and puts, debit spreads) shine when direction and timing are both right — decay punishes early entries. Debit spreads cost less than outright longs, and the short leg's decay offsets some of the long leg's.

Credit and income plays (cash-secured puts, covered calls, credit spreads, iron condors) shine in calm or gently trending markets with elevated implied volatility — you sell expensive premium. They suffer in sharp moves, exactly when undefined-risk versions turn dangerous.

Volatility plays (long straddle, long strangle) shine when the realized move beats what options implied. Their enemy is IV crush — buying before a known event means paying peak premium that collapses after.

Hedges (protective put, collar) shine when you must hold through uncertainty. Their cost is the drag in quiet months.

Frequently asked questions

What is the best options strategy for a beginner?

No universal best exists. Start small with a defined-risk, one- or two-leg structure — covered calls if you own 100 shares, cash-secured puts if you would buy the stock lower. Paper trade in the Builder first.

What is the difference between a debit and a credit strategy?

Debit: you pay to enter; max loss is the debit and the stock must move enough. Credit: you're paid to enter and profit if conditions stay favorable — but credit alone isn't safe; naked shorts can carry undefined risk.

How do I choose strikes and expirations?

Strikes show your forecast and risk tolerance: nearer strikes cost more but need less movement. For defined-risk spreads, 30–60 days is a common window — meaningful decay without the final week's gamma risk.

What does "defined risk" actually protect me from?

It caps the structural loss at a known amount. It doesn't stop you losing that amount, nor remove early-assignment or gap risk.

Should I hold a winning trade until expiration?

Not necessarily — many traders close credit spreads near 50% of max profit rather than waiting for the last, slowest dollars. Decide your exit before entry.

Can I lose more than I put in?

With defined-risk structures, no. With undefined-risk shorts, yes — a violent move can exceed the account balance and trigger a margin call.
