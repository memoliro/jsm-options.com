Basics · No jargon overload

What is an option? It’s a reservation ticket.

If you’ve paid to reserve sneakers, a console, concert tickets, or a house, you already get the idea.

Story 1: The $100 sneakers

Limited sneakers cost $100. You only have $5 today. You pay $5 to reserve the right to buy at $100 within 30 days.

That $5 is Premium. $100 is Strike. 30 days is Expiry. The sneakers are the Underlying.

Outcomes
  • Sneakers go to $150 → you still buy at $100. Gain about $45 after the $5 fee.
  • They fall to $70 → you walk away. Loss only $5.

That contract is a Call. CALL = right to BUY.

Story 2: House — Call vs Put

Call: fee now to lock the right to buy later at today’s price — useful if you expect price up.

Put: fee to lock the right to sell at a set price — useful if you expect price down (or as insurance).

Key: Buying an option is a right, not an obligation. You can walk away. Max loss on a long option = premium paid (before fees).

Story 3: Concert tickets

Face value $200, show in 30 days. You pay $20 for the right to buy at $200. If resale hits $400, you can still buy at $200. If resale is $100, you let the right expire and lose the $20.

Expiry is the deadline. After that, only intrinsic value remains (often zero if out of the money).

Five words to keep

  • Underlying — the asset (stock, index, sneakers).
  • Strike — agreed buy/sell price.
  • Premium — price of the option contract (quoted per share).
  • Expiry / DTE — how long the right lasts.
  • Call vs Put — call = right to buy; put = right to sell.

Long = buy the option. Short = sell the option.

Payoff picture (long call)

Below the strike at expiry you typically lose the premium. Above the strike, value rises. Rough breakeven ≈ strike + premium paid.

Example: $100 strike + $3 premium → about $103 at expiry to break even (before commissions). One contract = 100 shares, so $3 ≈ $300 cash.
