Covered calls for monthly income: how the trade really works

Search focus: covered call income · selling covered calls · covered call strategy · monthly income options · covered call risks

The covered call is the most-shared income trade in options — and the most misunderstood. Done properly, it converts shares you already own into cash premium, month after month. Done carelessly, it caps your winners, keeps your losers, and pays you a little while your stock falls a lot. This guide covers the real mechanics: what you sell, what you keep, what can go wrong, and when the trade actually fits.

What you are actually selling

One covered call means two things held together: 100 shares of a stock and one short call option against them. The call gives someone else the right to buy your shares at the strike price before expiry. You collect a premium for selling that right. That premium is yours to keep, whatever happens next.

It is called covered because the shares cover your obligation. If the stock rockets past the strike, you deliver shares you already own — you never have to buy them at the higher price. Compare that with a naked call, where a runaway stock can produce an almost unlimited loss.

The income math, with real numbers

Say you own 100 shares bought at $50 each ($5,000 total). You sell a 30-day call with a $52 strike for $0.80 per share. One contract covers 100 shares, so you collect $80 immediately.

Three endings, one month later:

  • Stock below $52: the call expires worthless. You keep the $80 and still own the shares. You can sell another call next month.
  • Stock at $52 or above: your shares are called away at $52. You made $2 per share on the stock plus $0.80 premium — $280 total on the position, minus any costs.
  • Stock falls to $45: the call expires worthless and you keep the $80 — but your shares lost $500. The premium softened the fall; it did not prevent it.

That third case is the one income posts skip. Premium is a cushion, not a floor.

Why it is not a salary

Some months there is no good call to sell. If implied volatility is low, or the only strikes with decent premium sit far below your cost, the honest move is to sell nothing. Forcing a trade every month — especially selling a strike below what you paid — turns a patient income strategy into a machine for locking in losses.

Think of covered-call income as irregular rent, not a paycheck. Good months pay more, quiet months pay nothing, and the stock itself still decides most of your return.

The risks, stated plainly

  • Capped upside. If your stock doubles, you still sell at the strike. The regret is real, and it is the price of the premium.
  • Full downside. You keep every dollar of a falling stock, minus the small premium.
  • Assignment surprises. Early assignment can happen, usually around dividends. Know your stock's ex-dividend date before you sell.
  • Tax and fees. Premiums and assigned sales can have tax consequences that vary by country. Keep records; this is education, not tax advice.

When a covered call actually fits

The trade fits when three things are true at once: you already like the stock enough to hold it, you expect a flat-to-mildly-rising month rather than a breakout, and the premium is worth the upside you give up. If any of the three is missing, hold the shares and wait — or look at a different strategy.

FAQ

Do I need exactly 100 shares to sell a covered call?

One standard contract covers 100 shares, so 100 shares is the usual starting point. With fewer shares the call would be partially uncovered, which most brokers restrict and which changes the risk completely.

What happens if the stock rockets past my strike?

Your shares are sold at the strike price. You keep the premium plus the gain up to the strike, and you miss the rest of the rally. That capped upside is the trade-off you accepted when you sold the call.

Is the premium guaranteed income?

The premium is yours once the sale fills — but future premiums are never guaranteed. Some months the only available premium is too small to justify capping your upside, and the right move is to sell nothing.

What happens at expiry if the stock is below my strike?

The call expires worthless, you keep the full premium, and you still own the shares. You can then decide whether to sell another call for the next cycle.
